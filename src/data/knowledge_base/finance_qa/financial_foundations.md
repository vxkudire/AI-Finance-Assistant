# Financial foundations

These original learning articles explain general concepts, not individualized financial, tax, or legal advice. Products, protections, and rules differ by country and change over time.

## Article 1: Setting useful financial goals
A useful financial goal names what the money is for, how much is needed, and when it is needed. Break a large goal into regular contributions and review progress when income, costs, or timing change. Separate near-term needs from distant goals: money needed soon usually has less time to recover from market losses. A goal is a planning tool, not a guarantee that a particular investment return will occur.

## Article 2: Understanding net worth
Net worth is the value of what you own minus what you owe at a given time. Assets may include cash, investments, and property; liabilities may include loans and credit-card balances. A simple, consistently updated list can show whether debt is shrinking or savings are growing. Net worth is only one measure of financial health: it does not show how easily assets can be accessed or whether monthly cash flow is sustainable.

## Article 3: Reading your cash flow
Cash flow is money coming in and going out over a period. Compare take-home income with recurring bills, flexible spending, debt payments, and saving. Reviewing actual transactions can reveal irregular costs or subscriptions that a rough estimate misses. If spending regularly exceeds income, identify changes that are realistic and sustainable rather than relying on a one-time fix. A positive monthly balance creates room for reserves and future goals.

## Article 4: Building a spending plan
A spending plan assigns expected income to priorities before the month begins and is compared with actual spending later. Categories can be broad or detailed; the best level is one you will maintain. Frameworks such as needs/wants/saving percentages are starting points, not universal rules. Housing costs, family responsibilities, location, and income stability may require different proportions. Adjust the plan when real spending differs, rather than treating a budget as a pass-or-fail test.

## Article 5: Automating savings thoughtfully
Automatic transfers can help savings happen consistently, especially when they occur after income arrives. Choose an amount that leaves enough for bills and a safe account balance, then monitor the transfer and fees. Keep separate goals distinct when that helps avoid using long-term savings for short-term spending. Automation is not “set and forget”: revisit it after a change in income, expenses, account terms, or priorities.
