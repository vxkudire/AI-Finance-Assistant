# Market mechanics and investing habits

These original learning articles cover general market mechanics. Specific order types and trading rules depend on the brokerage and exchange.

## Article 21: Understanding investment fees
Investment costs can include fund expense ratios, account charges, advisory fees, trading commissions, bid-ask spreads, and currency conversion costs. A small recurring fee can reduce the amount left invested over many years. Compare like with like: a lower-cost product is not automatically suitable if its risks, service, or holdings differ. Read the fee schedule and product disclosures, and ask which charges are ongoing versus one-time.

## Article 22: Regular investing and dollar-cost averaging
Investing a fixed amount on a regular schedule buys more units when prices are lower and fewer when they are higher. This is often called dollar-cost averaging. It can make contributions systematic, but it does not guarantee a profit or protect against a falling market. Compared with investing a lump sum immediately, spreading contributions means some money may remain uninvested for a time. The right approach depends on cash needs and comfort with risk.

## Article 23: Market orders and limit orders
A market order seeks prompt execution but does not guarantee the exact price, particularly in a fast-moving or illiquid market. A limit order sets a maximum purchase price or minimum sale price, but it may not execute at all. Order rules and available instructions differ among brokers and markets. Before placing an order, check whether it is for the intended security, quantity, account, and time period.

## Article 24: Liquidity and investment access
Liquidity describes how readily an asset can be converted to cash without a large delay or price concession. Publicly traded securities may be easier to sell than property or private investments, but trading volume and market stress matter. Some products impose lockups, withdrawal limits, or redemption fees. Match liquidity to when money may be needed and keep near-term spending separate from assets that may be hard to sell.

## Article 25: Time horizon and market volatility
A time horizon is the period before money is expected to be used. A longer horizon may allow more time to recover from market declines, but it does not remove risk or guarantee recovery. A short horizon leaves less time to wait out losses. Revisit the investment mix as a goal approaches, and avoid assuming that historical performance predicts what will happen over the remaining period.
