# Investing instruments

These original articles introduce common investment concepts. They do not recommend securities or promise returns; products and investor protections vary across markets.

## Article 11: Risk and return
Investment return is uncertain, and higher expected return generally comes with greater risk or variability. Risk includes more than short-term price changes: an investor may face inflation, default, liquidity, concentration, or currency risk. A projected return is an estimate, not a promise. Before investing, consider when the money will be needed, how much loss could be tolerated, and whether the investment's risks are understood.

## Article 12: Compounding over time
Compounding occurs when returns remain invested and may themselves earn returns in later periods. The effect depends on time, contribution amount, fees, taxes, and actual returns, which fluctuate. A calculation using a constant rate is only an illustration; real investments do not grow at a smooth fixed rate. Regular contributions can add to invested capital, but they cannot eliminate the possibility of loss.

## Article 13: Inflation and purchasing power
Inflation is a broad rise in prices that reduces what a unit of currency can buy over time. A nominal return is measured before adjusting for inflation; a real return accounts for changes in purchasing power and may also need to account for taxes and fees. Cash can be useful for near-term spending and stability, but holding all long-term savings in cash may expose purchasing power to inflation.

## Article 14: Stocks and ownership
A share of stock represents an ownership interest in a company. Shareholders may benefit if the company grows or distributes profits, but share prices can fall and dividends are not guaranteed. Company-specific events and broader market conditions both affect value. Owning a single company's shares concentrates risk; investors should understand how a stock fits with their overall goals and ability to withstand losses.

## Article 15: Bonds and lending
A bond generally represents a loan to an issuer, which promises specified payments under stated terms. The issuer may fail to pay, and a bond's market price can change before maturity. Interest-rate changes, inflation, credit quality, currency, and liquidity can affect bond values. A bond is not automatically risk-free, and the protections and priority of bondholders depend on the instrument and issuer.
