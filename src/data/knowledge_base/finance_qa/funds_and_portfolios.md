# Funds and portfolio basics

These original articles explain ways investments are packaged and managed. Availability, fees, tax treatment, and regulation depend on the product and jurisdiction.

## Article 16: Mutual funds and exchange-traded funds
A mutual fund or exchange-traded fund pools money from investors to hold a portfolio of assets. Funds can follow an index or use active management, and they may hold stocks, bonds, or other investments. An exchange-traded fund trades on an exchange during the day; mutual-fund transactions generally occur at a calculated end-of-day value. Neither structure guarantees diversification, low cost, or positive returns; review holdings, fees, and risks.

## Article 17: Index investing
An index tracks a defined group of securities according to stated rules; investors generally cannot buy the index itself. An index fund seeks to follow an index, so its results can differ because of fees, trading, and tracking error. Index investing is not risk-free: the fund can decline with the market it follows, and an index may be concentrated in particular companies or sectors.

## Article 18: Diversification
Diversification spreads exposure across investments whose returns may not move in exactly the same way. It can reduce the effect of one company's poor performance, but it cannot prevent losses when broad markets fall. Owning many funds is not necessarily diversified if they hold the same underlying assets. Look through fund holdings and consider asset types, industries, regions, and other relevant exposures.

## Article 19: Asset allocation
Asset allocation is the mix of broad investment categories in a portfolio. It is often shaped by the investor's time horizon, need for growth or income, financial capacity for loss, and willingness to accept fluctuations. No single mix is right for everyone, and a suitable allocation can change as goals or circumstances change. Understand the role and risk of each category instead of choosing a mix solely from past returns.

## Article 20: Rebalancing a portfolio
Rebalancing brings a portfolio back toward a chosen allocation after market movements cause the mix to drift. Some investors use calendar reviews; others use preset thresholds. Rebalancing may involve trading costs, taxes, or time out of the market, depending on account and location. It does not improve returns reliably or prevent losses. Define a method in advance and consider whether new contributions can restore the mix with fewer transactions.
